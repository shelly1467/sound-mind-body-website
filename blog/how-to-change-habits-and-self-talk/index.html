EVERYDAY HABITS

How to Change Your Habits and Self-Talk

To change a habit, choose a specific cue, a small action you can repeat, and a setup that makes it easier. Pair the action with realistic self-talk, then use what happens to adjust the plan.

Maybe you want to read instead of automatically opening an app, begin a task you keep avoiding, or respond to a mistake without calling yourself a failure. Each goal becomes easier to plan when you name the exact moment you want to handle differently.

This guide helps you build and test that plan. The examples and worksheet are practical suggestions, not a treatment program or a promise that every habit will change in the same way.

Start with one situation you can recognize

A habit is a learned pattern of behavior that can become relatively automatic in a familiar context. Self-talk is the language you use internally to describe yourself and what is happening. You can work on an action and the words around it, while recognising that thoughts do not always arrive by choice.

Instead of setting a broad goal such as “be more disciplined,” describe a moment:

  • “When I sit down for my afternoon break, I open my phone without deciding what I want to do.”
  • “When I open a task that feels unclear, I switch to something easier.”
  • “When I notice an error, I tell myself I cannot do anything right.”

Look for a cue you could notice again: finishing an activity, arriving somewhere, seeing an object, or encountering a particular situation. You do not need to monitor every thought. Choose one ordinary pattern that matters to you and feels manageable to work with.

Connect a clear cue to a small action

Write a simple plan: “When this happens, I will do this.” Make the action specific enough that you can tell whether you did it.

“When I put my afternoon drink on the table, I will read one paragraph of the book beside it” gives you more to work with than “I should read more.” One paragraph is a starting size you can adjust, not a scientifically required amount.

Researchers call plans linking a situation to a response implementation intentions. Gollwitzer and Sheeran's 2006 review (new tab) found that these plans helped goal attainment across varied studies. That does not mean every plan succeeds or that completing a planned action makes it an automatic habit.

Choose a version that fits an ordinary day, including the days when time or energy is limited. If the action requires a long preparation, several decisions, or perfect motivation, make the first step smaller.

Let your surroundings carry some of the work

Before asking yourself to try harder, check what your surroundings are asking you to do. Is the book in another room? Is the file difficult to find? Is the next step unclear?

  • Put the useful item where the cue happens. Leave the book beside the chair or a notebook beside the place where you plan tomorrow.
  • Prepare the first step. Leave a short note naming the next action, such as “Find the date for the first paragraph.”
  • Add a little distance from an unwanted default. If practical, put your phone out of reach during the moment you have chosen for reading.
  • Plan for a constraint you already know about. If mornings are unpredictable, choose a cue later in the day rather than repeatedly relying on an unavailable time.

These are options to test, not rules about how your home or routine should look. A shared space, disability, caregiving responsibilities, or changing work hours may call for a different arrangement. A workable plan respects those realities.

Use self-talk that helps you identify the next step

Try describing the problem without turning it into a verdict about yourself. “I have not started because the instructions are unclear” points to a question you can ask. “I am useless” gives you very little practical information.

A useful writing prompt is: “What happened? What can I do next?” For example:

  • “I missed the planned reading break. I can leave the book ready for the next one.”
  • “I am unsure how to begin. I can identify the part I need explained.”
  • “I made an error. I can correct this detail without judging everything about myself.”

You do not have to replace doubt with a glowing statement. Wood, Perunovic, and Lee's 2009 research (new tab) found that repeating a strongly positive self-statement left some participants with low self-esteem feeling worse in the short term. The study did not test the exact phrases above or prove that neutral wording works better for everyone. It is a reason to notice your response instead of forcing words that feel upsetting.

If you would like more background on supportive statements, read what affirmations are and how to use them.

Three examples of a complete plan

These are illustrative examples, not accounts of particular people's results. Borrow the structure and change the details to fit your day.

1. Choose reading before automatic scrolling

  • Cue: I set down my drink at my usual afternoon break.
  • Small action: I read one paragraph before deciding whether to check my phone.
  • Environment: The book is open at the right page; the phone is somewhere less convenient to reach.
  • Self-talk: “I can choose how to use the first part of this break.”
  • What to track: Did I read the paragraph when that break happened?

The aim is a deliberate choice at one point in the day. You do not need to turn it into a rule against using your phone.

2. Start a task that feels too vague

  • Cue: I begin the time I set aside for the task.
  • Small action: I open the file and write one question that needs an answer.
  • Environment: A shortcut to the file and the relevant instructions are ready.
  • Self-talk: “I can make the task clearer before trying to finish it.”
  • What to track: Did I identify the question, and is the next step clearer?

If the task depends on someone else's information, the next useful action might be asking for clarification rather than pushing yourself to work without it.

3. Respond to an everyday mistake more fairly

  • Cue: I notice a correctable error, such as forgetting an attachment.
  • Small action: I name the correction that is needed and take that step.
  • Environment: If the error recurs, I add one relevant item to my existing checklist.
  • Self-talk: “The attachment is missing. I can send it now.”
  • What to track: Did I return to the correction, even if a harsh thought appeared first?

You can take responsibility without requiring yourself to feel calm immediately. The thought appearing is different from the response you choose afterward.

Track opportunities, obstacles, and adjustments

A brief note can help you see whether the plan fits your life. At the chosen moment, record only what is useful:

  1. Did the cue occur? If the planned situation did not happen, there may have been no opportunity to practice.
  2. Did I do the chosen action? Record yes, partly, or no without adding a judgment about yourself.
  3. What got in the way? Be concrete: no materials, an interruption, unclear instructions, low energy, or a step that was too large.
  4. What will I adjust? Change the cue, simplify the action, prepare an item, or ask for support.

For example: “The break happened; I did not read; the book was upstairs; tomorrow I will put it beside the chair.” That note suggests an adjustment. “I have no willpower” does not tell you what to change.

After you have tried the plan in several real situations, look for a recurring obstacle. This is a suggested review point, not a research-based deadline. You can track less often or stop tracking if it becomes another source of pressure.

What to do when the plan slips

Return to the next appropriate opportunity rather than trying to compensate with an oversized effort. If the plan repeatedly does not happen, investigate the fit:

  • You forget: Choose a more noticeable cue or put the reminder where the action happens.
  • You remember but cannot start: Check whether the step is clear, small, and possible with the resources you have.
  • Your schedule changes: Revise the context instead of treating the old plan as a test of character.
  • The self-talk feels false or harsh: Use a factual sentence, leave the words out, or pause the exercise.

In Lally and colleagues' 2010 study (new tab), volunteers repeated a chosen everyday behavior in a consistent context and reported how automatic it felt. Automaticity generally increased with repetition, with considerable variation in timing. Missing one opportunity did not materially disrupt the process in that study. It did not establish a fixed timetable for changing self-talk or every kind of habit.

You do not need a 21-day or 66-day countdown. A more useful question is whether your chosen action is becoming easier to remember and carry out, and whether it still serves a goal you care about.

Write your own small plan

Complete these five lines in a notebook or wherever you keep reminders:

  1. When this specific situation happens: ______
  2. I will take this small action: ______
  3. I will make it easier by preparing: ______
  4. A fair sentence I can use is: ______
  5. If the plan does not fit, I will review: ______

Start with one plan. Keep what proves useful, make changes when your circumstances change, and allow progress to include asking for help.

When more support is needed

This guide offers general education for everyday routines and self-reflection. It is not a treatment for a mental health condition or a substitute for medical care. If a pattern is causing significant distress, putting you at risk, or interfering with daily life, seek appropriate professional support rather than relying on a habit worksheet alone.
