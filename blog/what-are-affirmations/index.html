AFFIRMATIONS

What Are Affirmations? Examples & How to Use Them

An affirmation is a short, intentional statement you use to practice a supportive way of speaking to yourself. It might be a reminder of what matters to you, an encouragement before a difficult task, or a kinder response to a familiar criticism.

You do not have to pretend everything is perfect. A useful place to begin is one honest sentence: “I can take one steady step today.” The sentence leaves room for a hard day while giving you something practical to return to.

Examples of affirmations for everyday life

Choose words that fit your situation. These examples are starting points, not statements you have to believe on command.

  • Confidence: “I can take one step even when I feel unsure.”
  • Self-love: “I am learning to treat myself with more kindness.”
  • After a mistake: “I can learn from this without turning against myself.”
  • A busy morning: “I can choose one thing to begin with.”
  • Rest: “I am allowed to pause and notice what I need.”
  • Body respect: “My body deserves care, including on difficult days.”
  • Gratitude: “I can notice something I appreciate without denying what is hard.”
  • Motivation: “A small, useful action is enough to start.”

Affirmations are often grouped under confidence, self-love, abundance, healing, morning routines, or sleep. Those labels describe a theme, not a promised result. Words about abundance can focus on gratitude and thoughtful choices; they cannot guarantee money or opportunities. Words about healing can express care without claiming to cure an illness.

If gratitude is the theme you want to explore, you can continue with our guide to everyday gratitude practices.

How to choose words that feel believable

An affirmation that feels far away from your experience may bring up frustration or pressure. You are allowed to change the wording. Try “I am learning,” “I can practice,” or “I am open to” when a stronger statement feels uncomfortable.

  • Instead of “I never make mistakes,” try “I can take responsibility and keep learning.”
  • Instead of “I am confident in every situation,” try “I can prepare and ask for help when I need it.”
  • Instead of “Everything is wonderful,” try “This is difficult, and I can choose one caring next step.”

These gentler versions are suggestions to try, not a scientifically proven formula. Notice your own response. A neutral reminder such as “I have options” may feel more useful than an enthusiastic declaration.

Leave room for difficult feelings. Sadness, anger, disappointment, and doubt do not mean you have failed at positive thinking. You can acknowledge a feeling and still speak to yourself respectfully.

How to use affirmations: a simple daily practice

You can try the following practice in a minute or two. The time is a convenient starting point, not a required dose or a promise of results.

  1. Notice the moment. Ask, “What am I saying to myself, and what do I need right now?” You might need encouragement, a pause, a clearer plan, or support from someone else.
  2. Choose one believable sentence. Keep it short enough to remember. “I can begin with one small step” is enough.
  3. Read, write, or say it gently. Use whichever format feels comfortable. There is no need to force a feeling or repeat the sentence a particular number of times.
  4. Pair it with one small action. If the sentence is about preparation, open the document and write a first line. If it is about care, take a break or ask for what you need.
  5. Check whether it helped. Did the words feel supportive, neutral, or upsetting? Keep, soften, or set aside the sentence based on that response.

For example, after putting off a task, you might write: “I feel overwhelmed. I can work on one part for five minutes.” Then choose the part. The affirmation becomes a prompt for a manageable action rather than another standard you have to meet.

A familiar moment, such as opening your journal or sitting down with a morning drink, can be a reminder to practice. If you miss a day, return when it is useful. You do not need a perfect streak.

What does research say about affirmations?

Research links open in a new tab, so this guide stays open.

The word affirmation is used for different practices. That matters when reading claims about the science.

Values-based self-affirmation

In much of the psychological research, self-affirmation means reflecting on personally important values, often through a writing exercise. In their 2014 review, Cohen and Sherman (new tab) describe how these exercises can help people respond less defensively to some threats to their sense of self. Effects depend on the context. This research does not show that repeating any positive phrase will produce the same benefits.

A values reflection might begin with: “Kindness matters to me. When have I acted kindly, and what would kindness look like today?” That is different from simply repeating “I am amazing.”

Repeating positive self-statements

Wood, Perunovic, and Lee's 2009 experiments (new tab) found that repeating a very positive self-statement left some participants with low self-esteem feeling worse in the short term. Participants with high self-esteem showed a limited benefit. The study does not establish that every affirmation is harmful or helpful; it shows why one approach may not suit everyone.

Brain research has limits

Cascio and colleagues' 2016 study (new tab) examined a values-based self-affirmation task and activity in brain systems involved in self-related processing and reward. It did not demonstrate that everyday affirmation phrases can instantly rewire the brain, erase difficult experiences, or treat a mental health condition.

The practical takeaway is to be curious about your experience and cautious about sweeping promises. A phrase can be a personal reminder without being a proven treatment.

What affirmations can and cannot do

You might use an affirmation to pause, name a value, or remember a next step. You may also find that a conversation, a change in your routine, practical help, or rest is more useful that day.

Affirmations cannot guarantee confidence, health, financial success, or a particular outcome. They do not remove responsibility for action, and you are not responsible for hardship because you failed to think positively.

If a phrase makes you feel worse, you can stop using it. If distress is persistent, intense, or interfering with daily life, consider support from a qualified mental health professional. You do not have to manage everything through self-talk.

An optional place for music

If you enjoy listening while you journal, stretch, or take a quiet break, you can explore our affirmation music. Music is one way to make space for reflection. Choose it because you enjoy the experience, rather than expecting it to change a belief or produce a health result. Reading or sitting quietly is just as welcome.

Begin with one honest sentence

Some days, supportive words may feel comforting. Other days, they may feel distant. Both experiences are allowed. Start with a sentence you can live with, connect it to a small action, and give yourself room to adjust.
